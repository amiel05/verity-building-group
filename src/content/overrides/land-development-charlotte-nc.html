Understand the property

Investigate before the design becomes expensive.

01

Legal and planning

  • Boundary and topographic survey information
  • Recorded easements and legal access
  • Zoning, setbacks, overlays, and use restrictions
  • HOA or architectural-review requirements
02

Physical conditions

  • Slope, soils, drainage, and stormwater paths
  • Tree canopy and clearing considerations
  • Existing structures or environmental concerns
  • Shoreline or watershed conditions where applicable
03

Infrastructure

  • Water, sewer, well, and septic feasibility
  • Power, gas, communications, and service routes
  • Driveway geometry and construction access
  • Off-site improvements or shared facilities

A beautiful lot can carry costly constraints. Early review should identify what is known, what must be verified, who is qualified to verify it, and how each finding may influence the concept. Property-specific advice should come from the appropriate surveyor, engineer, attorney, environmental professional, utility provider, authority, or other specialist.

A response to each site

Waterfront, infill, and acreage each ask different questions.

01

Lake Norman properties

Waterfront views can compete with solar orientation, privacy, access, slope, shoreline limitations, and outdoor-living goals. The correct response is property-specific and should be verified before purchase or plan completion.

02

Charlotte infill and larger tracts

Infill work may involve demolition, close neighbors, mature trees, limited staging, and contextual design. Larger sites shift attention toward access length, private utilities, drainage networks, and long-term maintenance.

Considering land and architecture together creates opportunities that neither discipline sees alone. The goal is not simply to make a structure fit, but to create an approach, arrival, orientation, and landscape that feel inevitable.

Before you build

Good decisions start
with good questions.

Should I involve a builder before buying land?

Early involvement can help identify construction and access questions during the buyer’s feasibility period. It does not replace legal, survey, engineering, environmental, or other professional due diligence.

What documents are useful for an initial review?

A current survey, listing or parcel information, recorded restrictions, utility details, geotechnical or environmental reports, concept plans, and any prior jurisdiction correspondence can all be useful.

Can every waterfront lot support the home I imagine?

No. Buildable area, setbacks, slope, access, utilities, drainage, shoreline requirements, and neighborhood standards may limit placement or scale. Appropriate specialists should verify the specific property.

What is the difference between site work and vertical construction?

Site work prepares and serves the property through activities such as clearing, grading, drainage, utilities, roads, and foundations. Vertical construction generally refers to the building itself, though the scopes must be coordinated.

How should contingency be planned?

The amount depends on information quality, site risk, design maturity, and contract structure. Less-investigated land generally carries more uncertainty; the project team should establish a transparent allowance or contingency approach.
